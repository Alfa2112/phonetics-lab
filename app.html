History of Phonetics

People have studied speech sounds for more than two thousand years. The IPA symbols on every card in this lab are the result of that long effort to write down how we talk.

  1. Around the 4th century BCE

    The Sanskrit grammarian Panini describes in detail where and how speech sounds are produced in the mouth. It is one of the earliest systematic accounts of articulation.

  2. 1569

    The English writer John Hart argues that English spelling no longer matches speech and proposes a spelling that does. Spelling reform becomes a long debate.

  3. 1867

    Alexander Melville Bell publishes Visible Speech, a set of symbols that draw the position of the lips and tongue for each sound.

  4. 1877

    Henry Sweet publishes his Handbook of Phonetics and develops Romic, an alphabet of Latin letters for writing speech. It becomes a base for the IPA.

  5. 1886

    Paul Passy and a group of language teachers in Paris found the association that is later named the International Phonetic Association. They want learners to see pronunciation on paper.

  6. 1888

    The first International Phonetic Alphabet is published. Its rule is simple: one symbol for each sound, and one sound for each symbol.

  7. 1917

    Daniel Jones publishes the English Pronouncing Dictionary, which records Received Pronunciation in IPA. His cardinal vowels inspire the vowel chart you will use in this lab.

  8. 1989 to today

    The association revises the chart at its Kiel convention in 1989 and keeps updating it. Learner dictionaries from Oxford, Cambridge and Longman print IPA beside each word.

Why the IPA was created

Spelling is a poor guide to sound. The same letters can say different things, and different letters can say the same thing.

though /ðəʊ/ · through /θruː/ · tough /tʌf/ · cough /kɒf/

Why it helps you

Indonesian spelling is mostly regular, but English is not, so you cannot always guess how a word sounds. IPA removes the guess. It also separates words that look alike.

read /riːd/ · read /red/

Which English is this?

This lab follows standard British pronunciation, the style recorded by Daniel Jones and used in many learner dictionaries. Your device voice is set to British English when it is available.
